Human influence and multilingual discourse in an AI-agent social network

V Bansal · C² Lab (Computational Communication Research Lab), UC Davis (PI: Martin Hilbert)

Research Undergraduate research · Jan – Jul 2026 4 languages

2026

Moltbook is a social network built for AI agents. Agents, mostly running on the OpenClaw framework, post, comment, and vote, and people are supposed to only watch. In practice, many accounts that present as agents are run by humans. That makes Moltbook a rare setting for studying how people and machines influence each other inside the same conversation.

The lab project

A social network analysis of Moltbook, built around three questions:

  • Interaction. How do agents interact with one another, and what structure does the network of those interactions take?
  • Detection. Can humans posing as agents be identified from how they post and the tone they use?
  • Influence. When humans steer a conversation, how does that shift the overall tone of the discussion around them?

My contribution: multilingual analysis

  • Led the cross-lingual analysis of posts written in Chinese, Russian, French, and English.
  • Compared the four language groups on three dimensions: which political topics come up, the general tone of the conversation, and how technical the discussion gets.

Key finding

  • Chinese-language conversations were significantly more technical than the other three groups, centered on coding and other technical topics.

The work brought together computer science, cognitive science, and social science: computational methods applied to questions about language, influence, and identity in a community where it is not always clear who is human.

Grounded quiz generation from student notes: cutting LLM hallucination with validation and retrieval

V Bansal · NoteParrot (founder & lead engineer)

Applied Jun 2024 – present 65% fewer hallucinated questions

2026

NoteParrot turns a student's own notes (PDFs, photos of handwritten pages, pasted text, or YouTube lectures) into ten-question practice quizzes. It is built on retrieval practice, one of the most reliable findings in memory research. Its prompting draws on two memory principles, retrieval cues and encoding specificity, so questions cue material the way it appears in the student's own notes. The hard requirement is grounding: every question has to be answerable from the uploaded notes, not from what the model already knows.

System

  • Ingestion. One router turns every input into plain text: pdfplumber for PDFs, Tesseract OCR for photos, and transcript chunking plus summarization for YouTube. Unsupported file types fail before any model call.
  • Generation. An orchestrator plans each job (how many questions, whether the notes contain math), calls GPT-4o at temperature 0.7 with a six-line positional output format, parses by line position, and requests replacements until it has ten valid questions, up to a retry cap.
  • Validation. Each question is checked against a JSON schema (four choices, exactly one correct, a real explanation), deduplicated, and rendered through MathJax so no raw LaTeX reaches a student. The system fails closed: a broken question is discarded and logged, never shown.
  • Retrieval for long uploads. Notes are chunked by section with overlap, embedded with OpenAI embeddings into pgvector (every search filtered to that quiz), queried once per section heading so every topic is covered, and reranked with a cross-encoder before generation. Short uploads skip this and use the full notes.
  • Provenance. Every question stores the chunks and source file it came from, so an audit grades it against the exact passage that produced it.

What went wrong, and why

  • Ungrounded questions. Where the notes had gaps, the model filled them in from its own knowledge.
  • Lost in the middle. On long uploads, middle sections got no questions, matching known attention effects in long contexts.
  • Answer-length cue. Students learned that the longest option was usually correct and stopped reading. A validator rule now keeps all four choices within a similar length band.

The first two shared one cause, what context the model sees, and retrieval fixed both. The third was a bias in how distractors were written, fixed in validation.

Evaluation

  • LLM-as-judge on a fixed test set of real notes, with a four-part rubric: answerable from the notes, correct key, explanation consistent with the notes, structurally complete. The judge was checked against hand-graded questions before it was trusted.
  • The same test set graded before and after the changes: 65% fewer hallucinated questions.
  • A weekly production audit samples recent questions and grades each one against its stored source.
  • 1,000+ registered users and 5,000+ OpenAI calls a day at peak.

Methods

GPT-4o RAG pgvector Cross-encoder reranking LLM-as-judge OCR Django PostgreSQL

Applying learning science to AI-generated instruction

V Bansal · StarSpark AI (Applied AI Engineer)

Applied Mar 2026 – present 2,000+ lectures, 95% pass rate

2026

StarSpark generates lecture content for AP courses. Alongside building that system, I lead the team's research on the question underneath it: which learning strategies actually help students, and how can an AI system put them into practice?

Research

  • Reading group. Lead a weekly research group for the team. Each week we read a paper, discuss and critique its methods and findings, and work out how it should change what we build next.
  • Learning tiers and strategies. Research learning tiers and learning strategies from the literature, and how to implement them in AI-generated lectures.
  • Experiments. Turn those ideas into A/B tests that compare implementations in the product by their effect on student engagement.

System

  • Built RAG pipelines on Google ADK that generate AP-aligned lecture content.
  • Built a standards validation layer and evaluation harness that scores every lecture against the AP standards it should cover: 95% of 2,000+ generated lectures pass.

CareCart: A Solution for College Students & Grocery Shopping Struggles

V Bansal, P Singh, R Li, C Chandu, A Ramachandran, A Zhang

Paper Human-Computer Interaction, UC Davis · Sep 2025 · 5 pages

2025

For many college students, grocery shopping is stressful: a limited weekly budget, the pressures of university life, and health needs ranging from medical conditions to food intolerances. CareCart is a student-centered platform that suggests affordable, diet-friendly alternatives and offers a community space for recipe sharing and peer support. The paper follows the human-centered design process from interviews to a tested high-fidelity prototype.

My role: lead author. I led the project end to end: the interviews and thematic analysis, the personas and journey maps, and the design and build of the full high-fidelity prototype.

Method

  • Needfinding. Semi-structured interviews with three UC Davis students, ten open-ended questions each, moving from meal planning to dietary restrictions, feelings about food, and experience with nutrition apps. Thematic analysis found six pain points, led by time pressure, confusing labels and hidden ingredients, and overwhelming nutrition apps.
  • Synthesis. Two personas (a time-pressed pre-med student, and a lactose-intolerant student on a tight budget) and a journey map of a rushed grocery trip.
  • Ideation. 20+ concepts, including barcode scanning, affordable swaps, recipe sharing, and meal planning, narrowed by affinity diagramming to four design principles: personalization, affordability, simplicity, and community.
  • Conceptual model. A "personal assistant" interface metaphor that knows your health needs and budget, plus a cooking-blog metaphor for community recipe sharing.
  • Evaluation. Lo-fi to hi-fi prototypes, tested with three users and analyzed thematically. The community feed, clean layout, fast scanner, and traffic-light ratings worked. The meal planner felt overwhelming, alternatives were too expensive, and mandatory reminders annoyed people, which drove the next round of changes.

The live CareCart platform grew out of this work and now covers 200,000+ scraped products, with recommendations for 30+ medical conditions.

Methods

User interviews Thematic analysis Affinity diagramming Personas Journey mapping Prototyping Usability testing

Pathfinding for Snow Blowing

V Bansal · ACT Lab, USC Viterbi (PI: Nora Ayanian)

Poster USC Viterbi SHINE 2021

2021

Coverage path planning for an autonomous snow-blowing robot: a JetBot (NVIDIA Jetson Nano) with a snow-blower attachment, tested on a model driveway with artificial snow. The robot has to know where it is even when markers may be buried, account for snow depth and density, and clear the whole area efficiently.

Approach

  • Localization. QR codes placed around the driveway act as landmarks. Scanning them lets the robot localize itself and replan its route.
  • Visual odometry. Monocular visual odometry from the onboard camera estimates the robot's trajectory between landmarks. It is what makes the QR-code system work, solves the single-agent path-finding problem, and is a base for full SLAM.
  • Snow depth. Estimated geometrically from the camera image using known heights and proportions, instead of physical depth markers.
  • Collision avoidance. A PyTorch vision model detects lines and objects in the camera feed as a safety layer. It was prototyped, then paused in the final system.
  • Planning. A finite-state machine drives a zig-zag (boustrophedon) coverage path between QR waypoints. That clears the driveway without a separate scouting pass, saving battery and time.

Methods

Coverage path planning Jetson Nano Visual odometry Finite-state machines OpenCV PyTorch

Multi-Robot Path-Finding

V Bansal · ACT Lab, USC Viterbi (PI: Nora Ayanian)

Research Poster Research intern, 2020 – 2021 · USC Viterbi SHINE 2020 30% faster planning

2020

Multi-agent path finding (MAPF): many robots share one space, each with its own goal, and all of them have to reach their goals quickly without colliding. It is the core problem behind warehouse robot fleets, and the ACT Lab tests its solutions on fleets of drones. This work approached MAPF with deep reinforcement learning, alongside classical search.

Environment

  • Grid-world environments built with OpenAI Gym and encoded as matrices (0 for a free cell, 1 for an agent, 2 for a goal).
  • A curriculum from single-agent path finding (SAPF), to many agents, to obstacle-dense maps and larger environments.
  • High-fidelity simulation environments to validate low-latency navigation in obstacle-dense scenarios.

Model

  • A PyTorch Advantage Actor-Critic (A2C) network. Each agent sees an observation window centered on itself, which passes through three convolution + ReLU layers and is then flattened into linear layers.
  • Two heads: the actor outputs a policy over five actions (up, down, left, right, stay), and the critic estimates the state value used to compute the advantage.

Training and results

  • Tracked loss, reward, average reward, and episode length. Reward stayed flat and negative for about the first hour (exploration), then rose and plateaued as the policy started exploiting what it had learned.
  • SAPF: after about 10 seconds of training, the agent reached its goal in three steps.
  • MAPF with obstacles: agents reached their goals without collisions and learned to cooperate, including a deadlock where two agents moved aside so a third could reach its goal.
  • Applied classical A* and depth-first search planners, and cut path-planning execution time by 30% by refining the search heuristics.

Methods

Reinforcement learning Actor-critic (A2C) PyTorch OpenAI Gym A* search Simulation
